## Preface (Pīṭhike)

Part 1 of the Ṛgveda Saṃhitā. Although we have already touched on the subject of the seers (ṛṣis) of the Ṛgveda in the introductory section, we wish to say a few things on that subject here. Kātyāyana, in his Sarvānukramaṇī, has given the etymology of the word ṛṣi as यस्य वाक्यं स ऋषिः (*yasya vākyaṃ sa ṛṣiḥ*). That is, the one whose utterance a sūkta-form sentence is, is the seer of it. But since an exact knowledge of these seers and their utterances was, by hereditary transmission, always possessed by all the people of the Vedic age, no sentence, according to the etymology above, indicates the name of its utterer at all. For that very reason the compilers of the Ṛgveda too had no need to state the list of these seers and their compositions in detail, as Kātyāyana's Sarvānukramaṇī does. But after many centuries the need was felt for an index of these seers, together with the deities, the application (viniyoga) and the metre of all these sūktas.

Āśvalāyana, in the Tarpaṇa chapter of his Gṛhyasūtra (3-4), gives briefly a list of the principal seers of the Ṛgveda. In it:

"Now the seers: (1) the Śatarcins, (2) the Mādhyamas, (3) Gṛtsamada, (4) Viśvāmitra, (5) Nāmadeva, (6) Atri, (7) Bharadvāja, (8) Vasiṣṭha, (9) the Pragāthas, (10) the Pāvamānyas, (11) the Kṣudrasūktas, (12) the Mahāsūktas"

— these are the divisions. Taking up this very list, Ṣaḍguruśiṣya, the commentator on Kātyāyana, discusses it. According to his discussion, all the seers of the first maṇḍala must be called Śatarcins; the seers of maṇḍalas 2 to 9, Mādhyamas; and the seers of the tenth maṇḍala, Kṣudrasūktas and Mahāsūktas. Śatarcin means one who is the seer of somewhat more or less than a hundred ṛks. The seers from the second maṇḍala up to the seventh are Gṛtsamada, Viśvāmitra, Vāmadeva, Atri, Bharadvāja and Vasiṣṭha, or members of their families. These are called Mādhyamas.

It is the opinion of Western scholars that the whole of the eighth maṇḍala is not the work of a single family. In Āśvalāyana's view this whole maṇḍala is to be understood as belonging to the Kāṇva line. There is some proper reason for both opinions. Āśvalāyana calls the authors of this maṇḍala Pragāthas. Of the total of 103 sūktas of this maṇḍala, the 11 Vālakhilya sūktas and the other 50 sūktas are also Kāṇva sūktas. Therefore on the whole this maṇḍala may be called the Kāṇva maṇḍala. Since the Pragāthas themselves belong to the Kāṇva family and are the authors of the first sūkta, it is natural that Āśvalāyana called them all together Pragāthas.

Since all the seers of the ninth maṇḍala are the authors of the Pavamāna sūktas, they are all together known as Pāvamānis. All of these are seers of the other maṇḍalas themselves; there are no other, new ones. The sūktas of these seers, addressed to the deity Soma, have been gathered in one place and called the ninth maṇḍala. The sūktas of the tenth maṇḍala all contain different subjects, some very important and others ordinary. Āśvalāyana calls the seers of these sūktas Mahāsūktas and Kṣudrasūktas. The commentator Ṣaḍguruśiṣya divides them thus: all the sūktas up to the sūkta नासदासीत् (*nāsadāsīt*), Ṛ. Saṃ. 10-129, are Mahāsūktas, and all the rest Kṣudrasūktas. Considering the length of this 129th sūkta and the importance of its subject, it and other large sūktas like it may also be called Mahāsūktas. This division by the commentator is very reasonable.

A division made in this way can be regarded only from a rough point of view; there is no such exact rule in assigning every maṇḍala to its own family. For example, the sūktas of Madhucchandas Vaiśvāmitra, which could belong to the third maṇḍala, and the sūktas of Medhātithi Kāṇva, which could belong to the eighth maṇḍala, are both contained in the first maṇḍala itself. The sūktas of Parāśara, the grandson of Vasiṣṭha, which should belong to the seventh maṇḍala, are also included in the first maṇḍala (Ṛ. Saṃ. 1, 65-76). Therefore that rule is not so exact. Probably the compilers began to divide according to the quantity of ṛks belonging to the various seers, and at first placed together those who were seers of 100 ṛks. Later it may have seemed to them that this method was not feasible. Therefore, from the second maṇḍala onward, they gathered together the sūktas of one principal seer and of those born in his family and set them apart as one maṇḍala each. After all this, the remaining ordinary sūktas and the small sūktas may have been gathered together to form the tenth maṇḍala.

In the group of seers of the tenth maṇḍala there are many royal seers (rājarṣis), beginning with Kavaṣa Ailūṣa; there are Vaiśyas named Vatsarpi and Bhalandana; a woman named Apālā, the daughter of Atri; and a seer named Ūrdhvagrāva who does not belong to the group of the Āryas.

For some sūktas the names of the authors have probably been forgotten. In such cases some imagined authors can also be seen. For example, Nārāyaṇa as author of the Puruṣasūkta, Vena as author of the sūkta Ṛ. Saṃ. 10-103; and similarly for some other sūktas, the principal names occurring in those very sūktas are indicated. If the rule यस्य वाक्यं स ऋषिः (*yasya vākyaṃ sa ṛṣiḥ*)

is to be followed, then even the sūkta containing the dialogue of Urvaśī and Purūravas would have to be called their work. But to say that they are the authors of that sūkta is not correct from the Western point of view.

In many cases, from the names of seers found in the respective sūktas, one must say that those sūktas are works of those seers' descendants. According to some books, only four gotras, (1) Bhṛgu, (2) Aṅgirasa, (3) Vasiṣṭha and (4) Kāśyapa, are the most ancient; some are of the opinion that probably these four alone were the first and original Aryan lineages of Bhāratavarṣa, and that all the other seers were born in these gotras.

The commentator Ṣaḍguruśiṣya, citing from the Purāṇas and from the Bṛhaddevatā the life stories of some seers, describes their charm. As described in the Ṛgveda itself, it is known that the seers Vasiṣṭha and Agastya were born of Mitra and Varuṇa, and that the manner of their birth has certain special features. Likewise, how Śyāvāśva, the grandson of the seer Atri, became a seer for that very reason after being rejected by his beloved, and in the same way the story of other seers such as Gṛtsamada, is charmingly described.

In sūkta 9-67 of the Ṛgveda Saṃhitā there is a peculiarity. It has 32 ṛks. It is understood that the first three are the work of Bharadvāja, then three of Kāśyapa, and likewise each following three ṛks are the work of Gautama, Atri, Viśvāmitra, Jamadagni and Vasiṣṭha. These are the seers who later became famous as the Saptarṣis. The last eleven are indicated as of Pavitra Vasiṣṭha. The last two ṛks of this sūkta are also like a phalaśruti. Western scholars are of the opinion that the compilers may have gathered together the ṛks of these seers and made them into one separate sūkta.

To publish such a Ṛgveda Saṃhitā, extremely ancient, extremely profound, extremely vast, and the basis of all the śāstras, in simple Kannada together with a critical translation is not an easy task. Even so, with the lofty aim that through such a great work all people, from the learned to the unlearned, may gain growth of knowledge and become worthy of the grace of God, His Highness Śrī Jayacāmarājendra Oḍeyar Bahadur, G.C.B., G.C.S.I., Lord of the throne of Karnāṭaka, a lover of Vedic learning, himself a scholar, beloved of his subjects and devoted to God, is encouraging, by his generous patronage, this publication, which requires much expense; this must be called the merit of the whole multitude of the people of Karnāṭaka.
