## Preface (Pīṭhike)
### Printed p. i
In the preface to the second part of this Ṛgveda, with the Sāyaṇa-bhāṣya and the Kannada translation, we said that from the third part onward the printing would proceed quickly. It is a matter of joy that the printing of the third and fourth parts has now been completed in a very short time, about two months, and has reached the hands of the readers. Since the printing is now going on simultaneously and without interruption in two or three presses, we have the assurance that in future too it will be printed as at present, that is, at the rate of one part a month.

In presenting the subject matter of the work, the same method that was followed in the earlier parts has been followed in this part also. Suitable upākhyānas (narratives) have been inserted here and there. Among these upākhyānas the Śunaḥśepa narrative is very important. Besides this upākhyāna having a ritual application at the coronation of kings and emperors, it is well known from the Śruti that if everyone who desires male offspring duly performs the hearing, recitation and so on of this upākhyāna, his desire will surely be fulfilled. Even now, when blessing someone for the gaining of male offspring, it is the custom to use as a blessing-formula the closing sentence of this upākhyāna in the Aitareya Brāhmaṇa: सहस्रमाख्यात्रे दद्याच्छतं प्रतिगरित्र एते चैवासने श्वेतश्चाश्वतरीरथो होतुः पुत्रकामा हाप्याख्यापयेरल्लभंते ह पुत्राल्लभंते ह पुत्रान् (*sahasramākhyātre dadyācchataṃ pratigaritra ete caivāsane śvetaścāśvatarīratho hotuḥ putrakāmā hāpyākhyāpayerallabhaṃte ha putrāllabhaṃte ha putrān*).

In the division of this Ṛgveda, for convenience of printing, one adhyāya has been printed in each part. Since the 64 adhyāyas in it are more or less equal in size, each part contains about 500 or 600 pages.

A word or two is necessary about the deities propounded in the Ṛgveda. Although many deities are praised in the Ṛgveda, such as Indra, Agni, the Aśvins, Uṣas, the Viśvedevas, Mitra, Varuṇa, Tvaṣṭā, Pūṣan, Bhaga, Aryaman, Āpaḥ, Viṣṇu, Rudra, the Maruts, Savitṛ, and the Vasus, Rudras and Ādityas, the sūktas addressed to Indra and Agni are the most numerous, and Indra and Agni have the greater prominence. The names of the gods spoken of in the Purāṇas and the like, such as Śiva, Mahādeva, Durgā, Kālī, Rāma and Kṛṣṇa, are not mentioned anywhere.

### Printed p. ii
Although it is said that the two words Śiva and Rudra denote one and the same deity, only the deity named Rudra is propounded. The words that denote that deity, such as Śiva, Śaṅkara and Mahādeva, are found specifically in the Purāṇas and the like, where many upākhyānas and histories are narrated to explain how those names came into use. Likewise Viṣṇu too is propounded in only five or six sūktas, and the words that denote Viṣṇu, such as Nārāyaṇa, Hari and Mādhava, are not in the Veda but are mentioned only in the Purāṇas and the like.

Each of the deities is regarded as chief; when each deity is praised, his qualities are described as supreme, and he is prayed to give us the things we need, such as food, wealth and houses. Yet since the Maruts are described as sons of Rudra, and the Ṛbhus as men who obtained divinity through the performance of good works, there is room to infer that there was some degree of gradation among the deities. Moreover, in the mantra नमो महद्यो नमो अर्भकेभ्यः (*namo mahadyo namo arbhakebhyaḥ*) (Ṛ. Saṃ. 1-27-13) it is evident that a distinction between great and small among the deities is accepted. The reasons why the deities have their respective names — that is, why Rudra is called Rudra, why Viṣṇu has that name, and why deities such as Agni, Indra and Āditya have their names — are explained in the Brāhmaṇa and in the Nirukta. Such explanation of meaning makes clear the nature and the prominence of the respective deities. The explanation concerning the deities will be given at length in the later parts.

We wish to say once more that the Veda is the foundation of all the śāstras and arts. The Veda is the basis of all the śāstras, such as architecture (śilpa), grammar, music, literature, Vedānta and dramaturgy. Among them, since the nature of the science of architecture is spoken of in many places in the Ṛgveda, many Ṛks of the Ṛgveda support the view that architecture is more ancient and more excellent than all the other śāstras. For there are Ṛks in plenty that describe the nature, making and use of many things: the plan of construction of the sacrificial altar, which is absolutely necessary for performing yajñas and yāgas; the ten sacrificial implements named स्पृः (*spṛḥ*), कपाल (*kapāla*), शूर्प (*śūrpa*), कृष्णाजिनं (*kṛṣṇājinaṃ*), शम्या (*śamyā*), ऊलूखल (*ūlūkhala*), मुसल (*musala*), दृषत् (*dṛṣat*) and उपला (*upalā*); sacrificial equipment such as स्रुक् (*sruk*), सृव (*sṛva*) and चमस (*camasa*); implements of war such as the chariot, the thunderbolt, armour, arrows and quiver; and household implements. From this alone the antiquity of this science is evident. It is said in the twentieth sūkta of the first maṇḍala that the Ṛbhus, disciples of Tvaṣṭṛ Prajāpati, made sacrificial implements and by that very means attained divinity. Men cannot attain divinity by ordinary deeds. Therefore, if these Ṛbhus attained divinity through works of craftsmanship and became worthy of worship like the gods, the greatness of this science cannot fail to be apparent to anyone.

### Printed p. iii
The fact that Tvaṣṭṛ made the thunderbolt for Indra is described in the Ṛks त्वष्टास्मै वज्रं स्वर्यं ततक्ष (*tvaṣṭāsmai vajraṃ svaryaṃ tatakṣa*) (Ṛ. Saṃ. 1-32-2) and मह्यं त्वष्टा वज्रमतक्षदायसं (*mahyaṃ tvaṣṭā vajramatakṣadāyasaṃ*) (Ṛ. Saṃ. 10-48-3), and the fact that the Ṛbhus made a chariot for the Aśvins in the Ṛk तक्षन्नासत्याभ्यां परिज्ञानं सुखं रथं (*takṣannāsatyābhyāṃ parijñānaṃ sukhaṃ rathaṃ*) (Ṛ. Saṃ. 1-20-3). In युवाना पितरा पुनः सत्यमंत्रा यजूयवः (*yuvānā pitarā punaḥ satyamaṃtrā yajūyavaḥ*) (Ṛ. Saṃ. 1-20-4) it is said that these Ṛbhus, by the practice of their craft, made their aged parents young again as before; this makes clear that the excellence of this science can accomplish any task. Ṛks that make known the greatness of this science are available in the Ṛgveda in plenty.

Those who know say that the science of music too is very ancient and that the Sāmaveda is its foundation. In the Ṛgveda itself singing is mentioned in many places. Ṛks such as गायंति त्वा गायत्रिणः (*gāyaṃti tvā gāyatriṇaḥ*) (Ṛ. Saṃ. 1-10-1) and देवत्तं ब्रह्म गायत (*devattaṃ brahma gāyata*) (Ṛ. Saṃ. 1-37-4) refer to singing. Since the Sāmaveda is itself a kind of singing, and since Śruti sentences such as अयज्ञो ना एष योऽसाम (*ayajño nā eṣa yo 'sāma*) — that is, a sacrifice without Sāma singing is no sacrifice at all — make well known the antiquity and excellence of the science of music.

To publish such a Ṛgveda Saṃhitā, very ancient, very profound, very extensive and the foundation of all the śāstras, in simple Kannada with a critical translation is no easy task. Yet with the weighty aim that through such a great undertaking all people, learned and unlearned alike, may gain growth in knowledge and become worthy of the grace of God, His Highness Śrī Jayachāmarājendra Wodeyar Bahadur, G.C.B., G.C.S.I., the lord of the throne of Karnataka, a patron of Vedic learning, himself a scholar, beloved of his subjects and devoted to God, is encouraging with his generous patronage the publication of this work, which requires great expense; this must be called the good fortune of the multitude of the people of Karnataka. I pray that the Lord of all, the Veda-Puruṣa, may bestow in abundance on such a generous-minded lord, on his family, on the princess (who bears the name of the mantra Gāyatrī, regarded as the chief among Vedic mantras), and on the whole multitude of his subjects, all worldly fruits such as long life, health and prosperity, and also spiritual knowledge that leads to the highest good.
