# Rigveda Samhita — Volume 30 (Kannada → English translation)

## Preface (Pīṭhike)

### Printed p. i

In this part the last four chapters of the Ṛgveda Saṃhitā are completed and the work comes to its end. As we indicated in the preface to the first part, this great work is contained in thirty parts comprising 25 thousand pages. It is no exaggeration to say that many learned scholars and researchers, Western ones even more than we Indians, understand well how great is the importance of this Ṛgveda, which is the foundation of all cultures, of the various sciences such as music and literature, and of the Vedānta doctrines. The Ṛgveda, regarded as the most ancient book in the world, and the other Vedas, the Vedic literature and the Upaniṣads have won the recognition and respect of all the civilised countries of the world. But in the research, publication and propagation of such rare and useful works we have not advanced beyond the Westerners. The main reason for this is that these profound works are in the Sanskrit language and are hard for ordinary people—even for Sanskrit scholars—to understand. Corresponding to this, it need hardly be said that the chief cause is the absence of books that present these works in the vernaculars, in a simple style, critically, in accordance with modern science and literature, together with a translation of the meaning.

The first to print this Ṛgveda in England, in the Devanagari script, after carefully examining the old manuscript copies, so that it might be useful to all, was the eminent Western scholar of Germany, Prof. Maxmuller. This work is in the Devanagari script and contains the Saṃhitā and Pada texts with accents, and the Sāyaṇa-bhāṣya separately for each Ṛk. On the basis of this copy, containing the pure text, other Western and Indian scholars have carried out many researches. Since then the Ṛgveda with Sāyaṇa's commentary has also been printed in Bombay and in Calcutta. But they were printed long ago and the copies have run out, so they are not now to be had. Recently, that is in 1933, the Vaidika Saṃśodhana Maṇḍala (Vedic Research Board) of Poona began to print the Ṛgveda with Sāyaṇa's commentary and finished it in four parts in 1946. This is the work generally available now. The price of these four parts is about 100 rupees.

In the works mentioned above the Ṛgveda Saṃhitā, the Pada text and Sāyaṇa's commentary are printed with accents. They contain neither criticism nor explanation of meaning. A Western scholar named Wilson, following Sāyaṇa's commentary, translated only the original text into English.

### Printed p. ii

Apart from a few notes here and there, it contains no extensive explanation or criticism. Even so, this work is useful to students and others. Many years ago in Poona some people printed the Ṛgveda under the name Vedārthayatna, with the Saṃhitā, the Pada text, and explanation in the Marathi language. But this work was not completed; it was printed only as far as the third aṣṭaka and then stopped. Although this work has explanation and criticism of each Ṛk to a small extent, it must be said that they are not enough. In Calcutta, under the leadership of one Satīśa Chandra Seal, M.A., B.L., the Ṛgveda was printed in instalments, useful to all people, with the Saṃhitā, Pada text and Sāyaṇa-bhāṣya, with extensive explanation and criticism in Sanskrit and in English, Hindi and Bengali translations, and various other useful matters. But the printing of this work too stopped at 50 instalments (not even one chapter being complete). One may guess that lack of funds is the reason why the printing of works stops in this way. Apart from these works, it must be said that no attempt has been made to print the Veda in the vernaculars in translation and critically.

Besides this, in English, in the series Sacred Books of the East under the leadership of Prof. Maxmuller, many works relating to Vedic literature and their translations have been printed by Western scholars. In the American Harvard University Series and in the Oxford University Press Series there are many Vedic works and literary works published by Western scholars. In German and French too many Vedic works with criticism and explanation have been published.

It is known to everyone that the translation and publication of such Vedic works, or profound works, is not possible without the generous encouragement and help of governments or universities. For the Ṛgveda with commentary first published (First Edition) by Prof. Maxmuller, the generous financial help of the East India Company was obtained. For the printing of the second edition (Second Edition) the generous patronage of the Maharaja of Vizianagaram was obtained. In Western countries such as America and Germany also, government help for printing such works is always there. In this country too the help and encouragement given by kings and maharajas of many principalities to the publication of Sanskrit works is not small. The maharajas of states such as Baroda, Indore, Kashmir, Kāśī and Travancore, and their governments, have helped greatly in the advancement of Sanskrit works by giving room for the publication of many works in series of their own. In the Mysore country too, through the institution called the Oriental Library (now the Oriental Research Institute), they have published the Kṛṣṇa-yajurveda called the Taittirīya Saṃhitā with the commentary of Bhaṭṭabhāskaramiśra, and other Sūtra works. But that work is now proceeding slowly.

### Printed p. iii

But for the last ten years, under the leadership of our ruler Śrī Jayachamarajendra Wodeyar Bahadur, G.C.B., G.C.S.I., and through his generous encouragement and help, many excellent works that until now existed only in Sanskrit and had not been published in Karnataka and other vernaculars have been translated into the Karnataka language and published under the name Śrī Jayachamarajendra Granthamālā; this is a matter most worthy of praise. In this series the 18 Purāṇas, the 18 Upapurāṇas, works such as the Vāsiṣṭha-Rāmāyaṇa, the Adbhuta-Rāmāyaṇa, the Śaṅkara-Saṃhitā and the Hālāsya-Māhātmya, the Ṛgveda Saṃhitā with translation of the meaning, many Vedic works such as the Mahāpūjākalpa, Gāyatrījapavidhi, Śivapūjāvidhi, Dakṣiṇāmūrtijapavidhāna and Rudramahanyāsa, many hymns composed by Śaṅkarācārya, and hundreds of other works have been printed with their meaning. The translation and printing of a countless number of works, which could not be accomplished even in about 50 years, has been finished under the leadership of the great Prince within a period of just 10 years. The Prince's exceeding interest and learning in Vedic works, in literature and in Vedāntic works, and his weighty good purpose that the benefit of these good works may reach others as well, have helped in accomplishing this. Although all these tasks were carried out with the help of many court scholars and other learned men, the Prince's unrivalled learning and priceless counsel, and his interest and disposition in reading through, from beginning to end, and criticising all the works printed under his patronage, and giving counsel on what matters should be added where and how, and in what manner they should be written, have been the chief cause of the works being published quickly. In the same way, the expectation that under the Prince's generous patronage the remaining three Vedas and other priceless works will soon be published cannot prove vain. On this occasion the aid given by our esteemed Government is also a point worthy of praise.

It will not be out of place to mention briefly here how we have written the arrangement of the translation of the present work, the Ṛgveda Saṃhitā. In the first part, by way of introduction, many matters relating to the Vedas were set out: the Vedas, their importance, their use, the divisions of the Vedas, the Vedic commentaries, the commentators, the Brāhmaṇa work, Āraṇyaka and Upaniṣad belonging to each Veda, the Pada text, the Anukramaṇikā, the seers, the metres, the Nirukta, the various deities, matters concerning those deities, the benefit arising from knowledge of the meaning of the Veda, and many other matters.

Thereafter, for each sūkta, the seer, deity and metre, the viniyoga and other matters connected with it are stated, and before each Ṛk the Saṃhitā text and Pada text are written with accents. Then the Sāyaṇa-bhāṣya for each Ṛk is added there itself. Although the Ṛgveda has many commentaries, such as the Skandasvāmi-bhāṣya, the Veṅkaṭamādhava-bhāṣya, the Udgītha-bhāṣya and the Mudgala-bhāṣya, they are now

### Printed p. iv

not available in full. Only a few portions are found here and there. Moreover, although the Skandasvāmi-bhāṣya, like Sāyaṇa's commentary, is accompanied for each Ṛk by explanation of the meaning, and by necessary stories, history, grammar and other matters as the context requires, the work is not available in full; therefore the useful matters in the one or two aṣṭakas that are available have been collected and added under the heading "Special Topics" (Viśeṣaviṣaya). Although the Veṅkaṭamādhava-bhāṣya has been printed in full, it has neither the detailed explanation nor the meaning following the syntactic connection that Sāyaṇa's commentary has. There is only the meaning of some difficult words and brief notes. Even so, since this commentary and the Skandasvāmi-bhāṣya are older than Sāyaṇa's, the views and opinions of these commentators cannot be disregarded. Therefore we have added them here and there. The Udgītha, Mudgala and other commentaries are likewise available only in a few portions here and there, so their full benefit cannot be obtained. Since only Sāyaṇa's commentary is available in full (for all four Vedas), and since it contains for each Ṛk the translation of the meaning, explanation, Nirukta, earlier history and other useful matters, we have followed Sāyaṇa's commentary. Others have done likewise. It is no exaggeration to say that, apart from Sāyaṇa's commentary, there is in the present day no other means of understanding the Vedas properly. Prof. Maxmuller collected this Sāyaṇa-bhāṣya, which existed in palm-leaf and handwritten copies, with great effort, examined it well with the help of scholars, and printed it in the Devanagari script. Of this work the second edition (Second edition) is freer from faults and more attractive than the first printing. Following this, we have incorporated Sāyaṇa's commentary in this work.

Then for each Ṛk we have added the Pratipadārtha (word-by-word meaning), the Bhāvārtha (gist of the meaning) and the English translation. All these follow Sāyaṇa's commentary. Then in the division called "Special Topics" the special matters relating to that Ṛk—that is, matters concerning the deities, the Nirukta, the derivation of the form of words and their various meanings, stories, earlier history, matters concerning the seers, the opinions of Eastern and Western scholars, relevant matters stated in other Vedas and works (with authority), criticism of the subject, and so on—are explained so that all may understand easily. Lastly, the grammatical derivation is written for each word of each Ṛk. Because words that have occurred once recur again and again, the grammatical derivation has been written only up to the end of the first Maṇḍala (12 parts). Since the occasion arose that the work had to be finished in thirty parts, it has been left off there. Besides these matters, many matters considered indispensable for explaining the meaning of the Ṛgveda have been collected and added here and there. A detailed account of the various deities found in the Ṛgveda and the other Vedas has been added at the end of the 5th part. Likewise the very important work called the Bṛhaddevatā, spoken by the sage Śaunaka, has been added with translation of the meaning and translation at the end of the 28th part. Various portions of the Nirukta, the ancient work spoken by the sage Yāska, which is the first attempt at explaining the meaning of the Ṛgveda and one of the six Aṅgas, have been added with explanation here and there on the relevant occasions. This work is older than three thousand years and is a most important authority (Authority). Without its help the meaning of the Veda cannot be understood. Besides this, the explanations and opinions of Western scholars on various subjects have been added on the relevant occasions.

A Ṛgveda Saṃhitā accompanied by such extensive criticism of subjects has not so far been published in any language of India. One may say confidently that the existence of such a work in the Karnataka language is of great help to the advancement of literature. Although the Ṛgveda Saṃhitā ends here, the work of printing six or seven further parts remains, containing the Aitareya Brāhmaṇa, the Aitareya Āraṇyaka and Upaniṣad, the Nirukta, an alphabetical index (Index) of various subjects, and so on, which belong with it. This work will be undertaken after the sanction of the Government is obtained.

It is our duty to indicate here the part played by the scholars who took part in the making of this work. The president of the committee called the Vedavimarśana Vidvanmaṇḍali, constituted for publishing this work, His Holiness Śrī Śrī Siddhaliṅga Svāmi of the Nāgaliṅga Maṭha, Mysore, took great care in this work and gave his priceless counsel and help. It may be said that this work began through his effort. But as this great man attained union with Śiva about two years ago, he could not see the fruit of his effort completed. Likewise we must mention with deep sorrow that another member of the committee, a scholar of the Mīmāṃsā system named Devara Bhaṭṭa, has also passed away.

Śrī Devaḍu Narasiṃhaśāstri, M.A., of Viśveśvarapura, Bangalore, has written a criticism of some Mīmāṃsā sūtras in this Ṛgvedabhāṣya-bhūmikā. Śrī G. N. Chakravarti, M.A., Lecturer in Sanskrit at St. Philomena's College, Mysore, has written most of the part called Pratipadārtha in this work, and some of the matter in the part called "Special Topics" supported by authorities such as the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa. Śrī Venkataramanachar, M.A., B.T., a scholar of literature and proficient in the Hindi language, has translated many matters relating to the Veda that are in English and Hindi. The court scholar Br. Śrī Lakṣmaṇācārya, a grammarian, and Br. Śrī G. Viṣṇumūrtibhaṭṭa, a grammarian of His Highness the Maharaja's Sanskrit Mahāpāṭhaśāla, have written the grammar part. Br. Śrī S. Sītārāmaśāstri, retired pandit of the Government Oriental Research Institute, has written some of the part called "Special Topics"

Since the translation and publication of this great work demand much money, such a task cannot be carried out without the help and co-operation of kings, maharajas and governments. Just as in the past the East India Company and the Maharaja of Vizianagaram gave generous financial help to Prof. Maxmuller and enabled him to publish the Ṛgveda in the Sanskrit language, so for the welfare of all the people who speak the Karnataka language and for the advancement of literature, in this great undertaking Śrī Jayachamarajendra Wodeyar Bahadur, G.C.B., G.C.S.I., lord of the throne of Karnataka, lover of his people, himself a scholar, devoted to Vedic learning and intent on devotion to God, together with his Government, took the greatest interest, and by his invaluable counsel and encouragement, help and co-operation, caused this work to be brought to completion without obstacle.
