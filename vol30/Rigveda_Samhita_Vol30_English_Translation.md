# Rigveda Samhita — Volume 30 (Kannada → English translation)

## Preface (Pīṭhike)

### Printed p. i

In this part the last four chapters of the Ṛgveda Saṃhitā are completed and the work comes to its end. As we indicated in the preface to the first part, this great work is contained in thirty parts comprising 25 thousand pages. It is no exaggeration to say that many learned scholars and researchers, Western ones even more than we Indians, understand well how great is the importance of this Ṛgveda, which is the foundation of all cultures, of the various sciences such as music and literature, and of the Vedānta doctrines. The Ṛgveda, regarded as the most ancient book in the world, and the other Vedas, the Vedic literature and the Upaniṣads have won the recognition and respect of all the civilised countries of the world. But in the research, publication and propagation of such rare and useful works we have not advanced beyond the Westerners. The main reason for this is that these profound works are in the Sanskrit language and are hard for ordinary people—even for Sanskrit scholars—to understand. Corresponding to this, it need hardly be said that the chief cause is the absence of books that present these works in the vernaculars, in a simple style, critically, in accordance with modern science and literature, together with a translation of the meaning.

The first to print this Ṛgveda in England, in the Devanagari script, after carefully examining the old manuscript copies, so that it might be useful to all, was the eminent Western scholar of Germany, Prof. Maxmuller. This work is in the Devanagari script and contains the Saṃhitā and Pada texts with accents, and the Sāyaṇa-bhāṣya separately for each Ṛk. On the basis of this copy, containing the pure text, other Western and Indian scholars have carried out many researches. Since then the Ṛgveda with Sāyaṇa's commentary has also been printed in Bombay and in Calcutta. But they were printed long ago and the copies have run out, so they are not now to be had. Recently, that is in 1933, the Vaidika Saṃśodhana Maṇḍala (Vedic Research Board) of Poona began to print the Ṛgveda with Sāyaṇa's commentary and finished it in four parts in 1946. This is the work generally available now. The price of these four parts is about 100 rupees.

In the works mentioned above the Ṛgveda Saṃhitā, the Pada text and Sāyaṇa's commentary are printed with accents. They contain neither criticism nor explanation of meaning. A Western scholar named Wilson, following Sāyaṇa's commentary, translated only the original text into English.

### Printed p. ii

Apart from a few notes here and there, it contains no extensive explanation or criticism. Even so, this work is useful to students and others. Many years ago in Poona some people printed the Ṛgveda under the name Vedārthayatna, with the Saṃhitā, the Pada text, and explanation in the Marathi language. But this work was not completed; it was printed only as far as the third aṣṭaka and then stopped. Although this work has explanation and criticism of each Ṛk to a small extent, it must be said that they are not enough. In Calcutta, under the leadership of one Satīśa Chandra Seal, M.A., B.L., the Ṛgveda was printed in instalments, useful to all people, with the Saṃhitā, Pada text and Sāyaṇa-bhāṣya, with extensive explanation and criticism in Sanskrit and in English, Hindi and Bengali translations, and various other useful matters. But the printing of this work too stopped at 50 instalments (not even one chapter being complete). One may guess that lack of funds is the reason why the printing of works stops in this way. Apart from these works, it must be said that no attempt has been made to print the Veda in the vernaculars in translation and critically.

Besides this, in English, in the series Sacred Books of the East under the leadership of Prof. Maxmuller, many works relating to Vedic literature and their translations have been printed by Western scholars. In the American Harvard University Series and in the Oxford University Press Series there are many Vedic works and literary works published by Western scholars. In German and French too many Vedic works with criticism and explanation have been published.

It is known to everyone that the translation and publication of such Vedic works, or profound works, is not possible without the generous encouragement and help of governments or universities. For the Ṛgveda with commentary first published (First Edition) by Prof. Maxmuller, the generous financial help of the East India Company was obtained. For the printing of the second edition (Second Edition) the generous patronage of the Maharaja of Vizianagaram was obtained. In Western countries such as America and Germany also, government help for printing such works is always there. In this country too the help and encouragement given by kings and maharajas of many principalities to the publication of Sanskrit works is not small. The maharajas of states such as Baroda, Indore, Kashmir, Kāśī and Travancore, and their governments, have helped greatly in the advancement of Sanskrit works by giving room for the publication of many works in series of their own. In the Mysore country too, through the institution called the Oriental Library (now the Oriental Research Institute), they have published the Kṛṣṇa-yajurveda called the Taittirīya Saṃhitā with the commentary of Bhaṭṭabhāskaramiśra, and other Sūtra works. But that work is now proceeding slowly.

### Printed p. iii

But for the last ten years, under the leadership of our ruler Śrī Jayachamarajendra Wodeyar Bahadur, G.C.B., G.C.S.I., and through his generous encouragement and help, many excellent works that until now existed only in Sanskrit and had not been published in Karnataka and other vernaculars have been translated into the Karnataka language and published under the name Śrī Jayachamarajendra Granthamālā; this is a matter most worthy of praise. In this series the 18 Purāṇas, the 18 Upapurāṇas, works such as the Vāsiṣṭha-Rāmāyaṇa, the Adbhuta-Rāmāyaṇa, the Śaṅkara-Saṃhitā and the Hālāsya-Māhātmya, the Ṛgveda Saṃhitā with translation of the meaning, many Vedic works such as the Mahāpūjākalpa, Gāyatrījapavidhi, Śivapūjāvidhi, Dakṣiṇāmūrtijapavidhāna and Rudramahanyāsa, many hymns composed by Śaṅkarācārya, and hundreds of other works have been printed with their meaning. The translation and printing of a countless number of works, which could not be accomplished even in about 50 years, has been finished under the leadership of the great Prince within a period of just 10 years. The Prince's exceeding interest and learning in Vedic works, in literature and in Vedāntic works, and his weighty good purpose that the benefit of these good works may reach others as well, have helped in accomplishing this. Although all these tasks were carried out with the help of many court scholars and other learned men, the Prince's unrivalled learning and priceless counsel, and his interest and disposition in reading through, from beginning to end, and criticising all the works printed under his patronage, and giving counsel on what matters should be added where and how, and in what manner they should be written, have been the chief cause of the works being published quickly. In the same way, the expectation that under the Prince's generous patronage the remaining three Vedas and other priceless works will soon be published cannot prove vain. On this occasion the aid given by our esteemed Government is also a point worthy of praise.

It will not be out of place to mention briefly here how we have written the arrangement of the translation of the present work, the Ṛgveda Saṃhitā. In the first part, by way of introduction, many matters relating to the Vedas were set out: the Vedas, their importance, their use, the divisions of the Vedas, the Vedic commentaries, the commentators, the Brāhmaṇa work, Āraṇyaka and Upaniṣad belonging to each Veda, the Pada text, the Anukramaṇikā, the seers, the metres, the Nirukta, the various deities, matters concerning those deities, the benefit arising from knowledge of the meaning of the Veda, and many other matters.

Thereafter, for each sūkta, the seer, deity and metre, the viniyoga and other matters connected with it are stated, and before each Ṛk the Saṃhitā text and Pada text are written with accents. Then the Sāyaṇa-bhāṣya for each Ṛk is added there itself. Although the Ṛgveda has many commentaries, such as the Skandasvāmi-bhāṣya, the Veṅkaṭamādhava-bhāṣya, the Udgītha-bhāṣya and the Mudgala-bhāṣya, they are now

### Printed p. iv

not available in full. Only a few portions are found here and there. Moreover, although the Skandasvāmi-bhāṣya, like Sāyaṇa's commentary, is accompanied for each Ṛk by explanation of the meaning, and by necessary stories, history, grammar and other matters as the context requires, the work is not available in full; therefore the useful matters in the one or two aṣṭakas that are available have been collected and added under the heading "Special Topics" (Viśeṣaviṣaya). Although the Veṅkaṭamādhava-bhāṣya has been printed in full, it has neither the detailed explanation nor the meaning following the syntactic connection that Sāyaṇa's commentary has. There is only the meaning of some difficult words and brief notes. Even so, since this commentary and the Skandasvāmi-bhāṣya are older than Sāyaṇa's, the views and opinions of these commentators cannot be disregarded. Therefore we have added them here and there. The Udgītha, Mudgala and other commentaries are likewise available only in a few portions here and there, so their full benefit cannot be obtained. Since only Sāyaṇa's commentary is available in full (for all four Vedas), and since it contains for each Ṛk the translation of the meaning, explanation, Nirukta, earlier history and other useful matters, we have followed Sāyaṇa's commentary. Others have done likewise. It is no exaggeration to say that, apart from Sāyaṇa's commentary, there is in the present day no other means of understanding the Vedas properly. Prof. Maxmuller collected this Sāyaṇa-bhāṣya, which existed in palm-leaf and handwritten copies, with great effort, examined it well with the help of scholars, and printed it in the Devanagari script. Of this work the second edition (Second edition) is freer from faults and more attractive than the first printing. Following this, we have incorporated Sāyaṇa's commentary in this work.

Then for each Ṛk we have added the Pratipadārtha (word-by-word meaning), the Bhāvārtha (gist of the meaning) and the English translation. All these follow Sāyaṇa's commentary. Then in the division called "Special Topics" the special matters relating to that Ṛk—that is, matters concerning the deities, the Nirukta, the derivation of the form of words and their various meanings, stories, earlier history, matters concerning the seers, the opinions of Eastern and Western scholars, relevant matters stated in other Vedas and works (with authority), criticism of the subject, and so on—are explained so that all may understand easily. Lastly, the grammatical derivation is written for each word of each Ṛk. Because words that have occurred once recur again and again, the grammatical derivation has been written only up to the end of the first Maṇḍala (12 parts). Since the occasion arose that the work had to be finished in thirty parts, it has been left off there. Besides these matters, many matters considered indispensable for explaining the meaning of the Ṛgveda have been collected and added here and there. A detailed account of the various deities found in the Ṛgveda and the other Vedas has been added at the end of the 5th part. Likewise the very important work called the Bṛhaddevatā, spoken by the sage Śaunaka, has been added with translation of the meaning and translation at the end of the 28th part. Various portions of the Nirukta, the ancient work spoken by the sage Yāska, which is the first attempt at explaining the meaning of the Ṛgveda and one of the six Aṅgas, have been added with explanation here and there on the relevant occasions. This work is older than three thousand years and is a most important authority (Authority). Without its help the meaning of the Veda cannot be understood. Besides this, the explanations and opinions of Western scholars on various subjects have been added on the relevant occasions.

A Ṛgveda Saṃhitā accompanied by such extensive criticism of subjects has not so far been published in any language of India. One may say confidently that the existence of such a work in the Karnataka language is of great help to the advancement of literature. Although the Ṛgveda Saṃhitā ends here, the work of printing six or seven further parts remains, containing the Aitareya Brāhmaṇa, the Aitareya Āraṇyaka and Upaniṣad, the Nirukta, an alphabetical index (Index) of various subjects, and so on, which belong with it. This work will be undertaken after the sanction of the Government is obtained.

## Sūkta 95 (Maṇḍala 10)

Introduction.

Urvaśī and Purūravas.

This episode, in the form of a dialogue between Urvaśī and Purūravas, is one of the narratives that set forth a supramundane truth by presenting worldly love and separation. Citing an itihāsa, Sāyaṇa has described the occasion of this story in the following way.

### Printed p. 2

> ऐळोर्वशीतिहासोऽत्र वैशद्याय प्रवर्ण्यते ।
> इत्युवर्श्यैळसंवादमिममेषोऽप्यसूचयदिति ॥
> इत्युर्वश्यैळसंवादमिममेषोऽप्यसूचयदिति ॥

(Sāyaṇabhāṣyapīṭhikā. Ṛ. Saṃ. 10-95)

Mitra and Varuṇa were infatuated by Urvaśī's beauty, and through the emission of their seed they became the cause of Agastya's birth; yet they did not fail to curse her. They cursed her, the divine maiden, saying that she should be born on the earth and be united with a mortal.

At the time when a king named Iḷa, together with the sons of Manu, was roaming in a forest for hunting, he entered the place where Umā, the daughter of the mountain-king, who worshipped Śiva with undivided devotion, was dwelling. As the result of that offence he was at once changed into a woman. Then, when he prayed to Śiva, Śiva told him that only Pārvatī had the authority to forgive that offence; thereupon Iḷa took refuge with Pārvatī. As the fruit of his repentance he obtained Pārvatī's forgiveness, and she granted that in every year he should remain a woman for six months, and only during the other six months might he have his own proper form. While he had the form of a woman, Budha, the son of Soma, was infatuated with her and consorted with her. As a result a son was born. He became famous as Purūravas, king of the city Pratiṣṭhāna.

Urvaśī, cursed by Mitra and Varuṇa, descended to the earth as a result of that curse, and while she was roaming she saw Purūravas. Purūravas too, astonished at her beauty, fell in love with her. When he desired union with her, she consented on a condition. "Only when Purūravas is in bed may Urvaśī see him naked and bear it. If she sees him in a state of nakedness at any other time, she will at once go back to her own abode: this is the first rule. Secondly, Purūravas must keep in his charge and protect the two lambs that belong to her": this is the second rule. Purūravas agreed to the condition.

The two lived together and enjoyed a happy life for four years. After four years, one night the bleating of the lambs was suddenly heard. Purūravas rose and looked, and saw that the gods were carrying them off. As he ran in pursuit of them, a flash of lightning appeared at once. Then the naked body of the naked Purūravas became visible to Urvaśī. Instantly she left him and went her way. Purūravas

### Printed p. 3

felt that he had been caught in a fault, and set out searching for her. Wandering with a distraught mind, he saw her sporting with her companions. When he begged her to return and join him, she used harsh words and rejected his prayer. Sāyaṇa states that this conversation between the two is the present narrative occasion. The same episode is told in the Bṛhaddevatā in this way:

> पुरूरवसि राजर्षावप्सरास्तूर्वशी पुरा ।
> न्यवसत्संविदं कृत्वा तस्मिन् धर्मं चचार च ॥

(Bṛ. De. 7-147)

Formerly Urvaśī cultivated love for Purūravas, made an agreement (condition) with him, and lived happily with him. (What that agreement was is not stated. The account here differs from what was said above.)

> तया तस्य च संवासमसूयन् पाकशासनः ।
> पैतामहं चानुरागमिंद्रवच्चापि तस्य तु ॥

(Bṛ. De. 7-148)

Indra could not bear that Purūravas, a mortal, should enjoy happiness with Urvaśī, a divine maiden. Seeing Purūravas occupying Indra's position and enjoying the rank that Indra ought to enjoy, he became jealous and wished to put an obstacle in its way.

> स तयोस्तु वियोगार्थं पार्श्वस्थं वज्रमब्रवीत् ।
> प्रीतिं भिंधि तयोर्वज्र मम चेदिच्छसि प्रियं ॥

(Bṛ. De. 7-149)

Then Indra addressed the thunderbolt that was near him: "O thunderbolt, if you have regard for me and wish to be of benefit to me, use your power and bring about a breach in the love of Urvaśī and Purūravas."**NOTE**

> तथेत्युत्वा तयोः प्रीतिं वज्रोऽभिनस्त्वमायया ।
> ततस्तया विहीनस्तु चचारोन्मत्तवन्नपः ॥

(Bṛ. De. 7-150)

The thunderbolt gave Indra its word that it would obey his command, and by its extraordinary power brought about a breach in the love of Urvaśī and Purūravas. Purūravas, estranged from Urvaśī, wandered about like a madman.

### Printed p. 4

> चरन् सरसि सोऽपश्यदभिरूपामिवोर्वशीं ।
> सखीभिरभिरूपाभिः पंचभिः पार्श्वतो वृतां ॥

(Bṛ. De. 7-151)

As he wandered thus, he saw Urvaśī sporting in a lake. She was sporting together with five female friends whose bodily endowment matched her divine beauty, and so Urvaśī's form shone even more brightly and she was captivating to the mind.

> तामाह पुनरेहीति दुःखात्सा त्वब्रवीन्नृपं ।
> अप्राप्याहं त्वयाद्येह स्वर्गे प्राप्स्यसि मां पुनः ॥

(Bṛ. De. 7-152)

As soon as he saw her, Purūravas addressed her and asked her to come back to him and give him the happiness of union. Unable to accept his call, she spoke thus, in great sorrow: "O Purūravas, you cannot get me back now. When we two meet again in heaven, let us enjoy happiness."

> आह्वानं प्रति चाख्यानमितरेतरयोरिदं ।
> संवादं मन्यते यास्क इतिहासं तु शौनकः ॥

(Bṛ. De. 7-153)

Yāska calls this narrative, which ends in the conversation of Urvaśī and Purūravas, a dialogue (saṃvāda); Śaunaka calls the same episode an itihāsa (story).

The author of the Nirukta, explaining the derivation of the word Purūravas, says:

> पुरूरवाः बहुधा रोरूयते । तस्यैषा भवति ।

(Ni. 10-46)

Because he cried out making a great noise, he became known by the name "Purūravas," one who makes much noise. Having explained this, he cites Ṛk 10. 95. 7 from the Ṛksaṃhitā and speaks about Purūravas. Since this episode conveys more than merely a story, the purport of the mantra cited by the Nirukta author will be given later. Sāyaṇa has briefly told the story of the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa and shown the connection between that story and the present episode. Before learning the details of that story, it is necessary first to know in which places the story of Urvaśī and Purūravas is told.

Apart from the Ṛksaṃhitā, this story is told in eight different contexts. Those eight occasions are as follows:

### Printed p. 5

1. Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 11-5-1-15.
2. Kāṭhaka (Yajurveda) 8-10-3.
3. In the commentary of Ṣaḍguruśiṣya on the Sarvānukramaṇī.
4. The story part of the Harivaṃśa, which follows the order of the Vāyu Purāṇa.
5. Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4-6-19.
6. Bṛhaddevatā (this account is given above).
7. Mahābhārata, Ādiparvan (69.21-27).

Purūravas was born in the womb of Iḷā. Iḷā herself stood to him in the form of father and mother; as far as we know, it is said that both parents of Purūravas were in the form of Iḷā.**NOTE** Purūravas, born of her who had such an extraordinary form, ruled as lord of thirteen islands in the midst of the sea. To put a stop to this ravage, Sanatkumāra once came from the divine world and gave him counsel. But Purūravas did not change his course. Since he went on persecuting holy men in this way, they became angry and cursed him severely. All his power was destroyed, and he became bereft of consciousness. Even so, he brought fire from the world of the Gandharvas for sacrifice and worshipped it. Together with this fire, which is threefold, he brought Urvaśī from the world of the Gandharvas. By her he had six children: 1 Āyu, 2 Dhīrmā, 3 Amāvasu, 4 Dṛḍhāyu, 5 Vanāyu and 6 Śrutāyu — these are the six sons.

"Purūravas's greed for wealth was exceedingly great. However much wealth he gained, he was never satisfied. Once, as he was roaming for hunting, he entered the Naimiṣa forest. In this sūkta no deity is praised. In the sūktas of the Ṛgveda, for the most part, it is prayer praising some deity that is chiefly found. Yet here and there there are other descriptions too: the creation of the world, exposition of principles, praises of kings' gifts, and exposition of other matters. Along with these there are some, such as dialogues relating to the love-play of women and men. Among these the chief are the dialogue of Yama and Yamī, the dialogue of Agastya and Lopāmudrā, the account of Svanaya, and the mutual dialogue of Purūravas and Urvaśī. For love-play

These mantras may have a hidden meaning other than the one that appears on the surface, or they may have been included here in order to teach a particular moral principle. In very ancient times, that is, when the sūktas of the Ṛgveda had not been divided as they now are, such sūktas and many other kinds of sūkta (with and without praise of deities) may have been current. When the sūktas of the Ṛgveda were arranged in a fixed order, its compilers may have left out many such sūktas, not including them in the Ṛgveda (on the ground that they were not required). Many grounds can therefore be given for the view that not all the sūktas in use at that time are included in the present Ṛgveda; since we shall examine this matter in another context, no explanation on it is needed here. (1) उर्वभ्यश्नुते उरु महत् यशोऽभिव्याप्नोतीति (*urvabhyaśnute uru mahat yaśo 'bhivyāpnotīti*). Uru means much fame or renown; she attains (abhyuśnute) it. This woman is widely famous for her great fame (here fame means beauty, charm and the like); this is why she gets the name Urvaśī.

(2) ऊरुभ्यां मैथुने धर्मे पुरुषं अश्नुते व्याप्तीति उर्वाशिनी सती उर्वशीत्युज्यते । (*ūrubhyāṃ maithune dharme puruṣaṃ aśnute vyāptīti urvāśinī satī urvaśītyujyate |*). Ūrū means thighs. By her attractive thighs, in the act of union, she pervades the man with embraces and the like and delights him. Therefore this woman gets the name Urvāśinī or Urvaśī.

(3) अथवा उरुः महान् अस्याः वशः कामः सेयमुरुवशिनी सती उर्वशीत्युच्यते । (*athavā uruḥ mahān asyāḥ vaśaḥ kāmaḥ seyamuruvaśinī satī urvaśītyucyate |*). Uru means much, great. Vaśaḥ means desire of love. Since this woman has very great amorous desire, she gets the name Uruvaśinī or Urvaśī. Thus three kinds of etymology are given for the word Urvaśī.

Now, to explain the meaning of the word Apsarāḥ, its etymology is given. Although this word is not read in this Ṛk, it had to be mentioned in passing while explaining the word Urvaśī; therefore its etymology is given here.

(1) अप्सराः । सा हि अप्सारिणी भवति । अपः पतति नित्यमेव सरति । तत्पभवत्वात् तदेव तस्याः प्रियमुदकं तस्मादप्पराः (*apsarāḥ | sā hi apsāriṇī bhavati | apaḥ patati nityameva sarati | tatpabhavatvāt tadeva tasyāḥ priyamudakaṃ tasmādapparāḥ*). Apsarāḥ means apsāriṇī. Apaḥ means water. She who moves over the water. Since water is very dear to this woman, the name Apsarāḥ has come.

(2) अथवा अद्भ्यः सृतेत्यप्सराः । अपि चैवमन्यथा स्यात् । अप्स इति एतत् रूपनाम । अप्सातेः प्रतिषेधपूर्वस्य प्सातेः भक्षणार्थस्य । तद्धि अप्सानीयं आदनीयं भवति । न हि तद्भक्ष्यते । किं तर्हि । आदर्शनीयं तदाभिमुख्यन स्थित्वा द्रष्टव्यमेव भवति चक्षुषा न पुनर्मुखेन भक्षयि तव्यमित्यर्थः (*athavā adbhyaḥ sṛtetyapsarāḥ | api caivamanyathā syāt | apsa iti etat rūpanāma | apsāteḥ pratiṣedhapūrvasya psāteḥ bhakṣaṇārthasya | taddhi apsānīyaṃ ādanīyaṃ bhavati | na hi tadbhakṣyate | kiṃ tarhi | ādarśanīyaṃ tadābhimukhyana sthitvā draṣṭavyameva bhavati cakṣuṣā na punarmukhena bhakṣayi tavyamityarthaḥ*). Apsaḥ is a name for form. The root psā means "to eat". *A-psā* means not fit for eating **NOTE**. Therefore

the form that is in this woman is not fit to be eaten, but is worthy of being well looked at with the eyes. That is, the intended sense is that this woman's form is very attractive.

(3) व्यापनीयं वा । व्यापनमर्हति । अप्लृ व्याप्तौ तस्य वा अप्सः, तद्धि नायनेन रश्मिना आत्मनः प्रत्ययाधानार्थं व्यापयितव्यं भवति । स्पष्टं दर्शनायेति शाकपूणिः ॥ (*vyāpanīyaṃ vā | vyāpanamarhati | aplṛ vyāptau tasya vā apsaḥ, taddhi nāyanena raśminā ātmanaḥ pratyayādhānārthaṃ vyāpayitavyaṃ bhavati | spaṣṭaṃ darśanāyeti śākapūṇiḥ ‖*). From the root aplṛ vyāptau the word apsaḥ is formed and gives the meaning "pervading". That is, the intended sense is that the form pervades so as to be clearly seen by all and is visible directly.

All these differences of meaning given above accord with the opinion of the teacher Śākapūṇi. Now another meaning of the word Apsarā will be given. It has been said before that apsaḥ means form. Now, according to the root rā dāne, the word rā means "to give"; or, since this form (beauty) has been given to her by Brahmā, the name Apsarā has come; this too is the meaning.

To make the meaning of these words clear, Yāska has given the etymology of the 10th Ṛk of this sūkta, विद्युन्नया पतंती (*vidyunnayā pataṃtī*). That is explained in the Special Topics of that Ṛk.

##### भावार्थ (Bhāvārtha)

Translation: In the eighth anuvāka of the tenth maṇḍala of the Ṛgveda-Saṃhitā, four sūkta-s ended in the preceding adhyāya. Now this sūkta beginning हये जाये (*haye jāye*), the opening sūkta of the fifth adhyāya, is the fifth sūkta in the eighth anuvāka. It has eighteen Ṛks; the metre is Triṣṭubh. The Anukramaṇikā says: "Aila Purūravas, from desire for Urvaśī who had formerly lived with him, approached her again and wished to take her back; she, not wishing it, rejects him; three are Urvaśī's…" The verses of this sūkta हये जाये (*haye jāye*) (Ṛk 1), इषुर्न श्रिये (*iṣurna śriye*) (Ṛk 3), यासु जूर्णिः (*yāsu jūrṇiḥ*) (Ṛk 6), कदा सूनुः (*kadā sūnuḥ*) (Ṛk 12), सुदेवो अद्य (*sudevo adya*) (Ṛk 14), अंतरिक्षप्रां (*aṃtarikṣaprāṃ*) (Ṛk 17), and the three beginning सचा यदासु जहतीषु (*sacā yadāsu jahatīṣu*) (Ṛks 8-10)—in all these nine Ṛks are the words of Purūravas, son of Ilā, so Purūravas is the seer of these Ṛks; the other nine Ṛks are the words of Urvaśī, so Urvaśī is the seer of those Ṛks. Whatever subject is set forth in the words of the two is, for the respective Ṛks, the deity; the viniyoga has been given before.

By the mere sight of her, Mitra and Varuṇa's semen was discharged, and it fell into a pot (a vessel, or a vessel holding the water called vasatīvarī) that was there… **NOTE**

…and the sage Pururavas, son of Ilā, and Urvaśī: there is an itihāsa concerning them, which will be told here in detail. In former times Mitra and Varuṇa, while consecrated at a sacrificial ground, saw there the celestial woman named Urvaśī. At the mere sight of her the semen of Mitra and Varuṇa was discharged, and it fell into a pot standing there (a vessel, or a vessel holding the water called vasatīvarī).

They cursed the person who had caused their discharge, that is Urvaśī, to be born on earth in human form and to experience the pleasures and pains natural to men. Meanwhile a king named Ila, hunting in a forest together with the sons of Manu, entered the grove called Devīkrīḍa. In that grove Pārvatī, sporting in love with Īśvara, was delighting that god with many moods. Because the Goddess (Pārvatī) sported there together with Īśvara, the grove came to be called Devīkrīḍa. While Pārvatī and Parameśvara were at their secret play in this grove, she had cursed it, wishing that no one should come there, so that any male who entered it would at once become a woman. Therefore King Ila, having entered that grove, at once became a woman, and wandering about in shame took refuge with Śiva, asking to be saved from this state. Śiva told the king that this was a work the Goddess herself must do, and that he should therefore please her and ask a boon. Accordingly the king took refuge with the Goddess and begged her to grant him manhood as before. The Goddess in compassion granted that the king should be a woman for six months of the year and a man for the other six months. While this went on, the king in woman's form was roaming the grove with female companions, celestial women, when Budha (son of the Moon) came there for some other reason, saw this beautiful woman Ilā, and was overcome with infatuation. Afterwards a king named Pururavas was born to Ilā by that Budha. Urvaśī saw this King Pururavas, who ruled in the city of Pratiṣṭhāna, and fell in love with him. Then a kind of agreement was made between Pururavas and Urvaśī: that except when lying in bed she would not see the king naked at any other time; that the king would keep her two lambs in his charge and guard them; and that if this were violated she would at once leave him and go. Having made this agreement with the king, the two lived happily, sporting with each other for four years. After four years had passed, one night the gods carried off the lambs that were in the king's keeping. Hearing the bleating of the lambs, the king said he would bring them back from the thieves who were carrying them off, rose from the bed and, without putting on clothes, set out in haste naked. At that moment a lightning flash shone very brightly, and Urvaśī saw the king naked. Saying that the agreement they had made earlier was broken, Urvaśī rose and went away to heaven. The king, returning and not finding Urvaśī in bed, searched for her everywhere and came to the shore of Lake Mānasa. There the king saw Urvaśī, who was sporting in the water in the middle of the lake with her companions, celestial women, and wishing to sport with her again,

### Printed p. 20
he entreated her in many ways, in tears. But Urvaśī did not agree to the king's words; she said, "What you say cannot be carried out; therefore give up your longing for me and go back." The subject of this conversation between King Pururavas and Urvaśī is what is stated in this sūkta.

The Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa of the Śukla Yajurveda also speaks thus on this matter:
उर्वशी हाप्सराः........................ताग्ं ह यं ज्ञात्वाभिपरोवाद हये जाये मनसा ॥ (*urvaśī hāpsarāḥ........................tāgṃ ha yaṃ jñātvābhiparovāda haye jāye manasā ‖*)
(Śa. Brā. 11-5-1 etc.)
Since we have given the meaning of these Brāhmaṇa passages on pages 7–9 of the introduction to this sūkta, there is no need to explain them again.

#### Ṛk 1

##### ॥ संहितापाठः ॥ (Saṃhitā-pāṭha)

> ह॒ये जाये॒ मन॑सा॒ तिष्ठ॑ घोरे॒ वचां॑सि मि॒श्रा कृ॑णवावहै॒ नु ।  
> न नौ॒ मंत्रा॒ अनु॑दितास ए॒ते मय॑स्कर॒न्पर॑तरे च॒नाह॑न् ॥ १ ॥

### Printed p. 21

##### ॥ पदपाठः ॥ (Pada-pāṭha)

> हये । जाये । मनसा । तिष्ठ । घोरे । वचांसि । मिश्रा । कृणवावहै । नु ।  
> न । नौ । मंत्राः । अनुदितासः । एते । मयः । करन् । परतरे । चन । अहन् ॥ १ ॥

##### ॥ सायणभाष्यम् ॥ (Sāyaṇa-bhāṣya)

> पुरूरवसो वाक्यं । जायां पश्यन्वदति । हये हे घोरे । अस्माकं
> दुःखजनकत्वात् । घोरकारिणि जाये मनसास्मदुपर्यनुरागवता मनसा युक्ता
> सती तिष्ठ । क्षणमात्रं संनिधावेव निवस ॥ हय इत्यस्य निघाताभावश्चांदसः ॥ किमर्थं
> संस्थानमिति तत्राह । वचांसि वाक्यानि मिश्राण्युक्तिप्रत्युक्तिरूपाणि स्वद्य क्षिप्रं वा
> कृणवावहै करवावहै ॥ कृवि हिंसाकरणयोः । धिन्विकृणो रच्चेत्युप्रत्ययः ॥
> किमर्थं वचसः करणमिति चेत् उच्यते । नावावयोर्मंत्रा रहस्यार्थ एते
> विवक्षिता अनुदितासोऽप्याह्रियमाणाः परस्परमसंभाष्यमाणा गुंफिताः संतः परतरे
> चनाहन् । चनेति निपातसमुदायश्चार्थे । अनेकेषु दिवसेषु चरमेऽप्यहनि मयः
> सुखनामैतत् । सुखं न करन् । कुर्वंति । अतः कृणावाहहा इति ॥

##### Gist of the bhāṣya

This is Pururavas' speech; seeing his wife, he speaks. "Hye" means "O" (hē): O fierce one, because you cause us sorrow. O wife who acts fiercely, stay, with a mind full of affection for us; remain close by for just a moment. The absence of nighāta for हय (*haya*) is Vedic. Why stay? He says: let us exchange speeches (vacāṃsi), mixed, in the form of question and answer, today or quickly. Why make speech? These secret thoughts (mantrāḥ) of us two, if not uttered though intended, being merely strung together without being spoken to one another, do not bring pleasure (mayaḥ, a word for happiness), even on the last of many days; people do not make it. Hence "कृणवावहै (*kṛṇavāvahai*)".

##### प्रतिपदार्थ (Pratipadārtha)

- हये घोरे जाये (*haye ghore jāye*)—O wife of hard heart
- मनसा (*manasā*)—with a mind showing affection for me
- तिष्ठ (*tiṣṭha*)—stand a little
- वचांसि (*vacāṃsi*)—words in the form of conversation
- नु (*nu*)—now
- मिश्रा (*miśrā*)—both of us together
- कृणवावहै (*kṛṇavāvahai*)—let us converse
- *nau ete maṃtrāḥ*—these feelings hidden within our hearts
- अनुदितासः (*anuditāsaḥ*)—if not spoken openly
- परतरे चन् अहन् (*paratare can ahan*)—even in the last days
- मयः (*mayaḥ*)—happiness
- न करन् (*na karan*)—do not produce

##### भावार्थ (Bhāvārtha)

Pururavas: O wife of hard heart, stay a little with a mind showing love for me. Let us now together exchange words in conversation. If the feelings hidden in our hearts are not spoken openly, they will not bring happiness even in the last days.

##### English translation
(Pururavas speaks :) Ho indignant wife, with mind (relenting) stay
awhile, let us now interchange discourse. These, our secret thoughts, whilst
unspoken, did not yield us happiness even at the last day.

##### विशेषविषयगळु (Special Topics)

Hayē—this word is used here in the sense of "हे (*he*)" (O), for addressing. Besides this Ṛk it is used in the following:
हये देवा यूयमिदापयः स्थ ते मृळत नाधमानाय मह्यं (*haye devā yūyamidāpayaḥ stha te mṛḷata nādhamānāya mahyaṃ*)
(Ṛ. Saṃ. 2-29-4)
हये नरो मरुतो मृळता नस्तुवीमघासो अमृता ऋतज्ञाः (*haye naro maruto mṛḷatā nastuvīmaghāso amṛtā ṛtajñāḥ*)
(Ṛ. Saṃ. 5-57-8)
It is used in eight Ṛks; nowhere else. **NOTE**

Ghore—Pururavas addresses Urvaśī as "ghore" to indicate that her mind, toward him, has shown pity yet is very cruel in nature.
Vacāṃsi miśrā kṛṇavāvahai nu—"Now let us mix our words" means: speaking to one another, let us exchange the thoughts in our minds. The meaning: I shall say what is in my mind; you say what is in yours.
Manasā tiṣṭha—O woman, stay for a while, with affection for me, in order to speak lovingly.
Na nau mantrā anuditāsaḥ—the secret matters we ought to have spoken have remained unspoken. Since we had not seen each other for a long time, secret conversation was not possible; so what ought to be said to each other has not been said.
Ete mayaskran paratare canāhan—though this secret conversation would be pleasing to us, since there was no opportunity to carry it on to the end (up to now), it has not been pleasant for us.

##### ॥ संहितापाठः ॥ (Saṃhitā-pāṭha)

> पुरू॑रवः॒ पुन॒रस्तं॒ परे॑हि दुराप॒ना वात॑ इवा॒हम॑स्मि ॥ २ ॥

##### ॥ सायणभाष्यम् ॥ (Sāyaṇa-bhāṣya)

> अनया तमुर्वशी प्रत्युवाच । एतैतया वाचा केवलया पुनःसंभोगरहितया किं कृणव । किं करवाव । अहं त्विदानीं त्वत्सकाशात्प्राक्रमिषं । अतिक्रांतवत्यस्मि ।
> अतिक्रमे दृष्टांतः । उषसामग्रियेव । बह्नामुषसां मध्येऽग्राग्रे भवा
> पूर्वोषाः प्राक्रमाद्यथाहमपीति । यस्मादेवं तस्माद्दे पुरूरवः त्वं पुनरस्मत्सकाशादस्तं गृहं परेहि । परागच्छ । मा ममाभिलाषं कार्षिः । तस्या दुर्ग्रहत्वमाह । अहं वात इव वायुरिव दुरापना दुष्प्रापा दुरापा नास्मि । दुरापा ना अहं
> त्वय्यैतर्ह्यस्मि पुनर्गृहानिहीति हैनैनं तदुवाचेति वाजसनेयकं । शत. ११-५-१-७ ॥

##### Gist of the bhāṣya

Urvaśī replied to him with this Ṛk. With this speech alone, devoid of renewed union, what shall we do? I have now moved away from you, gone beyond, like the foremost of the many dawns, who goes ahead at the front of them; so have I. Therefore, O Pururavas, go back from me to your home; do not have desire for me. He states her unattainability: I am like the wind, hard to attain (durāpanā). He cites the Vājasaneyaka: दुरापा ना अहं त्वय्यैतर्ह्यस्मि पुनर्गृहानिहीति हैनैनं तदुवाचेति (*durāpā nā ahaṃ tvayyaitarhyasmi punargṛhānihīti hainainaṃ taduvāceti*) (Śa. 11-5-1-7).

##### प्रतिपदार्थ (Pratipadārtha)

- एता वाचा (*etā vācā*)—by this our conversation
- किं कृणव (*kiṃ kṛṇava*)—what can we accomplish?
- उषसां अग्रिया इव (*uṣasāṃ agriyā iva*)—as the first-born dawn slips away so that no one can seize her
- अहं (*ahaṃ*)—I
- तव (*tava*)—from you
- प्राक्रमिषं (*prākramiṣaṃ*)—have moved far away, come so as not to fall in your grasp
- पुरूरवः (*purūravaḥ*)—O Pururavas
- पुनः (*punaḥ*)—again (without following me)
- अस्तं (*astaṃ*)—to the home
- परेहि (*parehi*)—go back
- अहं (*ahaṃ*)—I
- वातइव (*vātaiva*)—like the wind
- दुरापना (*durāpanā*)—not to be caught in your grasp
- अस्मि (*asmi*)—am

##### भावार्थ (Bhāvārtha)

Urvaśī: O Pururavas, what can we accomplish by this conversation? As the first-born dawn slips away so that none can hold her, I too have moved far from you, come so as not to fall in your grasp. O Pururavas, do not follow me again; go back home. Like the wind I have escaped, not caught in your grasp.

##### English translation
(Urvashi :) What can we accomplish through such discourse ? I have passed away from you like the first of the dawns. Return, pururavns, to your dwelling. I am as hard to catch as the wind.

##### विशेषविषयगळु (Special Topics)

This is Urvaśī's speech: O Pururavas, since we cannot again unite with each other, what use is the mutual conversation you propose? Will a dawn that has passed come again on that same day? Never possible. Just as it is not possible to catch the blowing wind,

### Printed p. 24
so I am not one you can get. Therefore give up your longing for me and go back to your house: this is the main meaning.

#### Ṛk 3

##### ॥ संहितापाठः ॥ (Saṃhitā-pāṭha)

> इषु॒र्न क्रि॒य इ॑षु॒धेर॑स॒ना गो॒षाः श॑त॒सा न रंहिः॑ ।  
> अ॒वीरे॒ क्रत्वा॒ वि द॑विद्युत॒न्नोरा॒ न मा॒युं चि॑तयंत॒ धुन॑यः ॥ ३ ॥

##### ॥ पदपाठः ॥ (Pada-pāṭha)

> इषुः । न । श्रिये । इषुधेः । असना । गोऽसाः । शतसाः । न । रंहिः ।  
> अवीरे । क्रत् । वि । दविद्युतत् । न । उरा । न । मायुं । चितयंत । धुनयः ॥ ३ ॥

##### ॥ सायणभाष्यम् ॥ (Sāyaṇa-bhāṣya)

> अनया पुरूरवाः स्वस्य विरहजनितं वैक्लव्यं तां प्रति ब्रूते । इषुधेः ।
> इषवो धीयंतेऽत्रेतीषुधिर्निषंगः । ततः सकाशादिषुरसनासनाय्कॆ प्रक्षेपुं न
> भवति श्रिये विजयार्थं । त्वद्विरहाद्युद्धस्य बुद्धावप्यनिधानात् । तथा रंहिर्वेगवानहं शत्रुसकाशाद्गोषास्त्रेषां शत्क्रोणां गवां संभक्ता नाभवं । तथा शतसाः
> शतानामपरिमितानां शत्रुधनानां संभक्ता नाभवं । किंचावीरे वीरवर्जिते क्रता
> राजकर्मणि सति न वि दविद्युतत् । न विद्योतते । न मत्सामर्थ्यं । किंच धुनयः
> कंपयितारोऽस्मदीया नरा उरोर् ॥ सुपां सुलुगिति सप्तम्या डादेशः ॥
> विस्तीर्णे संग्रामे मायुं । मायते प्रक्षिप्यत इति मायुः शब्दः ॥ कृवापाजीत्यादिनोण् ॥ सिंहनादं न चितयंत । न बुध्यंते ॥ चिती संज्ञानेत् । अस्माण्णिचि
> संज्ञापूर्वकस्य विधेरनित्यत्वाल्लघूपधगुणाभावः । छांदसो लज् ॥

##### Gist of the bhāṣya

With this Ṛk Pururavas tells her of his own distress born of separation. Iṣudhi is the quiver, where arrows are placed. From it, the arrow cannot be shot for victory (śriye), since because of separation from her his mind does not attend even to battle. Likewise, I, swift (raṃhi), have not become a sharer of hundreds of cows from the enemy, nor of the countless hundreds of enemy riches (śatasāḥ). Moreover, when the royal work is without a hero, my capacity does not shine forth. Further, my men, the shakers (dhunayaḥ), in the wide battle do not notice the lion-roar (māyu, that which is hurled forth); they do not perceive it. The remaining remarks concern grammar.

- श्रिये (*śriye*)—to gain victory
- असना (*asanā*)—to shoot
- न (*na*)—it is not possible
- गोपाः (*gopāḥ*)—to gain the enemy's cows **NOTE**
