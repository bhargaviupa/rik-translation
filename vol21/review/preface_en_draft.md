# Preface (Kannada → English)

In this part of the Ṛgveda-saṃhitā the sūktas of the sixth maṇḍala are completed, and the explanation of nineteen sūktas of the seventh maṇḍala is given. The seer of the sixth maṇḍala is Bharadvāja, and the seer of the seventh is Vasiṣṭha. Of these two seers, there are many grounds for saying that Bharadvāja lived hundreds of years before the seer Vasiṣṭha, and that Vasiṣṭha and Viśvāmitra were contemporaries. That Bharadvāja was the priest of a king named Divodāsa is indicated in such places as the Pañcaviṃśa Brāhmaṇa 15-3-7; Ṛgveda-saṃhitā 1-116-88**NOTE**{ಋಗ್ವೇದಸಂಹಿತಾ ೧-೧೧೬-೮೮ printed; reference as printed, please check}; 6-16-5; 6-31-4. The son of this Divodāsa was one named Pijavana. Vasiṣṭha was the priest of Sudāsa, who was his son or of his line. Viśvāmitra, too, was for a time the priest of this Sudāsa. Therefore it can be said beyond dispute that Bharadvāja was much earlier than Vasiṣṭha and Viśvāmitra.

In the last sūkta of the sixth maṇḍala there is a description of war and of the weapons and other equipment used in war. Among these weapons, bows and arrows are the chief. There is a description of what the bows were like, in what manner the arrows were made, and how they were used. From some mantras it becomes known that there was a custom of dipping the arrows in a poison prepared from the juice of a certain kind of herb and then discharging them. But it appears that such poisoned arrows were used only by the Dasyus, the former inhabitants of this country, who were the enemies of the Āryan people. It is a matter known to all that even now, in many countries, uncivilised forest-dwellers still follow this very practice. To obtain protection from such arrows, there are many descriptions of men fighting seated in chariots, wearing armours made of metal. Moreover, since in that time the king, the lord of the country, himself fought the enemies on the field of battle together with his army, a whole body of soldiers as bodyguards was always near the king for his protection. These were called Rathagopas. In wars there was the use of horses, chariots, warriors and the like, but the use of elephants and of swords is nowhere mentioned.

In the 18th, 33rd and 83rd sūktas of the seventh maṇḍala there is a description of a great war fought on the bank of the river Paruṣṇī between the famous king Sudāsa and ten kings named Simyu, Turvaśa, Druhyu, Kavaṣa, Pūru, Anu, Bheda, Śambara, Yadu and Vaikarṇa. In this war, as the result of the seer Vasiṣṭha, the priest of Sudāsa, praying to Indra, Sudāsa, by the help Indra gave, defeated these ten kings, many of whom were chiefs of the black people of this country called Dasyus; the details of this matter are given.

Although the seer Vasiṣṭha helped Sudāsa in many ways as his priest, it becomes known that after some time, for some other reason, hostility arose between Sudāsa and Vasiṣṭha. Or else, after the time of Sudāsa, hostility may have arisen between his sons and the Vasiṣṭhas. For it is said in the Taittirīya Saṃhitā of the Yajurveda that the sons of Sudāsa killed the sons of the seer Vasiṣṭha; that, desiring to avenge it, the seer Vasiṣṭha wished for sons who would have the power to conquer the sons of Sudāsa, performed the sacrifice suited to that, obtained sons, and conquered the sons of Sudāsa:

(Kṛ. Saṃ. 7-4-7-1**NOTE**{ಕೃ. ಸಂ. ೭-೪-೭-೧ printed; the text above says Taittirīya Saṃhitā; abbreviation ಕೃ. read letter for letter})

This is what is stated. That is, when his sons had been killed by the Saudāsas (the sons of Sudāsa), Vasiṣṭha wished that offspring might be born to him again and that with the help of those sons he might conquer the Saudāsas; he realised (as seer) the mantra beginning ⟦ಏಕಸ್ಮಾನ್ನ ಪಂಚಾಶಂ⟧ and by its power performed the sacrifice. As its result sons were born to him, and with the help of those sons he conquered the sons of Sudāsa. So it stands.

The enmity between Vasiṣṭha and Viśvāmitra is described in one way in the Vedic works and in another way in the Purāṇas and the like. We have set out a detailed discussion of this matter in the introduction to the seventh maṇḍala and in the introduction to Ṛ. Saṃ. Part 16.

To publish such a Ṛgveda-saṃhitā, which is most ancient, most profound, most vast, and the foundation of all the śāstras, in simple Kannada together with a critical translation is not an easy task. Even so, with the lofty aim that through such a great undertaking all people, scholars and laymen alike, may gain increase of knowledge and become worthy of the grace of God, His Highness Śrī Jayachāmarājendra Oḍeyar Bahadur, G.C.B., G.C.S.I., the Lord of the Throne of Karnataka, a lover of Vedic learning, himself a scholar, beloved of his subjects and devoted to God, and his Government, by their generous patronage encourage this publication, which calls for great expenditure; this must be called the good fortune of the multitude of the people of Karnataka. For our generous-minded Lord, his family, the princesses, the prince bearing the auspicious auspicious name Śrīkaṇṭhadatta Narasiṃharāja Oḍeyar, and the whole multitude of subjects, I pray that Sarveśvara, who is the Veda-Puruṣa, may graciously grant, all the more abundantly, all wealth, such as long life, health and prosperity, which are the fruits of this world, and also spiritual knowledge, which is the fruit of the other.
